WEEK 01 · ESSENTIALS

Problems, relevance & obligations

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Sustainability is a constraint on engineering decisions

    A lower footprint is useful, but planetary thresholds ask whether the total activity remains within environmental limits. Keep the scale of the system visible when making a sustainability claim.

  • Assess all three pillars

    Environmental improvements can create economic or social trade-offs. For microfabrication, cheap or reliable chips alone do not establish sustainability; resource use and impacts also belong in the problem definition.

  • Commitments influence what counts as progress

    Global commitments and the EU taxonomy provide context for engineering choices. Distinguish an evidence-based assessment from an unsupported green claim.

WEEK 02 · ESSENTIALS

Assessment framework & functional unit

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Compare the same service

    The functional unit quantifies what the system delivers. Comparing one chip with one different chip is misleading if their performance, lifetime or service differs. Define the function before choosing quantities.

  • Boundaries determine what you leave out

    Map raw materials, manufacturing, use and end of life. State exclusions so that the comparison remains interpretable; a manufacturing-only result is not automatically a whole-life result.

  • Use a tiered assessment

    Start with screening and increase detail where it changes the decision. Data requirements grow with assessment depth, so prioritise likely hotspots instead of collecting every possible measurement.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Sketch the system on one page

Define one measurable functional unit for your actual project. Draw the main life-cycle stages, mark the boundary and list three important assumptions.

FINISH WITH

A functional unit and simple process-flow diagram for Task 1.

WEEK 03 · ESSENTIALS

Case study · Problem formulation

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Make the assessment answer one question

    Microfabrication offers several possible projects: a manufacturer, DTU’s facility, a specific process or high-purity chemicals. Use your group’s selected topic and keep the assessment question narrow enough to investigate.

  • Missing data is part of the case

    This is an investigative project rather than a textbook dataset. Record proxies, gaps and assumptions as you go; an apparently precise number is weak evidence if its inputs are poorly supported.

  • Task 1 is the assessment contract

    The problem statement, functional unit and boundaries set what later calculations mean. A clear scope now reduces rework when you quantify impacts in Task 2.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Stress-test your problem statement

Spend 30 minutes reviewing your Task 1 scope against the case brief. For each pillar, identify a motivation; then note one data source and one uncertainty for the main impact you plan to calculate.

FINISH WITH

A short list of scope corrections and data questions for your group.

WEEK 04 · ESSENTIALS

Carbon footprint & environmental indicators

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • An inventory becomes an impact through factors

    Multiply activity quantities by compatible impact factors and sum over the boundary. Check units carefully: kWh × kg CO₂e/kWh gives kg CO₂e, whereas a factor in grams needs conversion.

  • The comparison includes more than operation

    The mobility exercise compares production and use, illustrating why a cleaner operating phase can coexist with production burdens. Apply the same boundary discipline to chip manufacture and chip use.

  • Carbon is one indicator

    A climate result does not cover every environmental effect. Use carbon to find important contributions, while keeping other relevant impacts visible in the case assessment.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Complete one mobility comparison

Open the carbon-footprint mobility workbook and Tool instructions 1–3. Fill the highlighted inputs for the gasoline/electric comparison, inspect the largest contribution and compare against the solution.

FINISH WITH

One checked comparison, with a sentence explaining its main driver.

WEEK 05 · ESSENTIALS

Health dimensions

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Health effects need their own assessment

    Climate emissions alone do not describe human health burdens. The module connects inventory-based health impacts, disease burdens and effects on users. Keep the mechanism and indicator clear.

  • Include harms and benefits consistently

    The mobility exercise includes health effects of physical activity as well as burdens. An assessment can omit a major effect if it looks only at emissions from a process.

  • One inventory supports several indicators

    The mobility workbook contains hidden health and ecosystem columns. Expanding them reuses the inventory; it does not remove uncertainty in activity data or characterisation factors.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Explain one health trade-off

Use Tool instructions 4 and the health mobility workbook. Expand the impact columns, compare cycling with one vehicle option and write down the role of physical activity alongside production/use impacts.

FINISH WITH

A three-sentence explanation of why the health comparison can differ from a carbon-only comparison.

WEEK 06 · ESSENTIALS

Economic dimensions

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Purchase price is not life-cycle cost

    Life Cycle Costing includes costs over the chosen lifetime and boundary. An expensive efficient option can have lower total cost; record usage and price assumptions before comparing.

  • Sensitivity reveals decision drivers

    Changing electricity prices or lifetime can change which option is cheapest. Test influential assumptions rather than treating a single price scenario as a permanent answer.

  • MCDA makes trade-offs explicit

    Multi-Criteria Decision Assessment and AHP combine criteria using preferences or weights. The ranking depends on those choices, so explain them and check how robust the conclusion is.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Test one cost assumption

Open the LCC mobility exercise, fill the missing cost inputs and compare with the solution. Change one influential price or lifetime assumption and note whether the ranking changes.

FINISH WITH

A baseline and alternative scenario with one sentence on robustness.

View task & self-check →

WEEK 07 · ESSENTIALS

Planetary boundaries & absolute sustainability

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Better does not necessarily mean sustainable

    Relative assessment asks which option has lower impact. Absolute assessment compares impact with an allocated share of environmental capacity; both options can improve while still exceeding that share.

  • Allocation is a consequential choice

    Translating a planetary boundary to a product or activity requires a sharing principle. Human needs, population or other allocation choices imply different ethical assumptions and can change the result.

  • Compare like units and scales

    A footprint and its allocated boundary must describe compatible quantities, time periods and indicators. The ratio is only useful once those choices are explicit.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Interpret one absolute-assessment result

Use Tool instructions 7 and the mobility absolute-sustainability exercise. Identify the impact, allocated boundary and allocation rule for one option; explain what changes if the allowed share changes.

FINISH WITH

A short distinction between “lower impact” and “within the allocated boundary”.

View task & self-check →
MICROFABRICATION CONNECTION

Resume Task 2 after the autumn break. Record the allocation assumptions you may need for the final Microfabrication assessment.

WEEK 08 · ESSENTIALS

Resource management, scarcity & criticality

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Renewable resources can still be overused

    Sustainable extraction depends on regeneration, not merely on whether a resource is labelled renewable. Fisheries illustrate how management decisions affect future availability.

  • Extraction has wider impacts

    A sustainable yield does not guarantee a low biodiversity or environmental footprint. Assess the wider consequences of the extraction strategy as well as the quantity harvested.

  • Criticality is broader than physical scarcity

    The criticality reading considers resource risks and vulnerability. For microfabrication, a small quantity of a hard-to-substitute material may deserve attention alongside high-volume energy or water inputs.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Screen three resources in your project

List three relevant inputs such as electricity, water or a process material. For each, identify a supply or regeneration concern, a wider environmental concern and the quality of your evidence.

FINISH WITH

A three-row resource-risk screen that can inform the case discussion.

View task & self-check →
MICROFABRICATION CONNECTION

WEEK 09 · ESSENTIALS

Case study · Applying methods

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Trace every important calculation

    Task 2 needs a transparent connection from activity data to an impact measure. Label sources, units, assumptions and conversions so the group can explain the result.

  • Put the number in perspective

    Compare with a relevant process or consumable using a defensible basis. A large absolute figure and a large impact per functional unit answer different questions.

  • Quantification still needs all three pillars

    The brief asks for environmental, economic and social aspects and their balance. Discuss relevant limitations when data are insufficient rather than inventing precise indicators.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Audit one key calculation

Take your project’s main impact calculation. Check dimensions, trace each input to a source and try one lower/higher plausible value for the weakest input.

FINISH WITH

An auditable baseline, a sensitivity result and one precise question for the teaching team.

WEEK 10 · ESSENTIALS

Energy systems & decarbonisation

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Electricity impact depends on the mix

    Weight each technology’s life-cycle emissions by its share of production. The mix’s g CO₂e/kWh then combines with electricity demand; shares must sum to a coherent total.

  • Low operating emissions do not mean zero life-cycle impact

    Electricity technologies have construction and supply-chain impacts too. Use life-cycle factors consistently rather than comparing one technology’s operation with another’s full life cycle.

  • Intensity and total emissions are different

    A lower-carbon mix can still produce substantial emissions when demand is high. The module compares mix scenarios with climate-related boundaries, making scale central to the conclusion.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Change one electricity-mix scenario

Open Planetary CO2 boundary – Electricity Technology Mix. Inspect the baseline and test one change in technology shares while keeping the shares consistent; compare intensity and total emissions.

FINISH WITH

Two scenarios and a sentence on the role of demand versus technology mix.

View task & self-check →
MICROFABRICATION CONNECTION

Finish Task 2 calculations and annexes before 18 November. Keep the baseline and sensitivity assumptions visible.

WEEK 11 · ESSENTIALS

Circular economy

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Circularity is a strategy, not an impact result

    Extending use, recovery or recycling can change resource flows. Whether a strategy improves sustainability requires quantifying the resulting changes and trade-offs.

  • Specify what changes in the system

    Identify the material, process or service affected, the recovery rate and any extra energy or processing. A circularity proposal without those assumptions cannot support a defensible comparison.

  • Readiness affects implementation

    The module introduces ready2LOOP and the Circularity Strategy Scanner. Technical feasibility, organisation and other readiness conditions matter when turning a proposed strategy into practice.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Screen one circular strategy

Choose one relevant option for your actual microfabrication project: for example longer use, recovery of a process material or reuse. State the necessary assumptions, a potential benefit and an added burden.

FINISH WITH

A short strategy proposal with a condition under which it would fail to improve sustainability.

WEEK 12 · ESSENTIALS

Sustainability transitions

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Transitions involve connected systems

    Changing one technology can alter demand, infrastructure and behaviour. Use a systemic perspective to identify interactions that a narrow product comparison may miss.

  • Feedbacks and delays affect outcomes

    System-dynamics exercises explore how interventions work over time. A promising immediate change can have different longer-term consequences when reinforcing or balancing effects are included.

  • Interpret dimensions together

    The assigned assessment-interpretation reading supports presenting combined results. Keep conflicting indicators and uncertainty visible instead of hiding them in one unsupported sustainability score.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Map a small transition pathway

For one proposed improvement in your project, draw a causal chain with one feedback loop or delay. Add a three-column table of environmental, economic and social effects, including uncertainties.

FINISH WITH

One diagram and a balanced interpretation to help structure Task 3.

WEEK 13 · ESSENTIALS

Case study · Integrated assessment

Full weekly plan →
01 / UNDERSTAND

The ideas to keep

  • Integrate rather than repeat

    Task 3 combines prior scope and calculations into a multidimensional conclusion. Use Tasks 1 and 2 as foundations, then explain what they jointly establish about sustainability.

  • Evaluate safe operating space

    Discuss the case’s allocated share and global significance where supportable. Make the allocation and scale assumptions explicit; uncertain data should limit the strength of the conclusion.

  • Explain systemic trade-offs

    Consider circular-economy opportunities and interactions among the three pillars. Show where a proposed improvement shifts burdens or depends on wider system conditions.

02 / PRACTISE30–60 min · Suggested

Review the conclusion against four criteria

Read your draft conclusion against critical sustainability evaluation, systemic perspective, uncertainty and integration of Tasks 1–2. Identify one unsupported claim and one important limitation to fix.

FINISH WITH

A short revision list and focused questions for the week-13 teaching team.
